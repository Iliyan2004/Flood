Abstract

Flood-risk decisions for assets, infrastructure and cities need four answers at once: where the water will go, what it will cost, which protection pays back, and whether a warning will arrive in time. In practice these come from separate tools, data formats and teams, so scenarios are tested rarely and results go stale. We present SAILAB, a platform that answers all four in one workflow for Pakistan. A two-dimensional shallow-water solver (LISFLOOD-FP 8.2, local-inertial scheme) runs on the GPU for any area drawn on a map, and a terrain-based instant preview screens scenarios first. Simulated depth and speed are turned into a damage class for every building, a loss in Pakistani rupees and an annual average loss. A mitigation module tests embankments, drains, retention basins and nature-based options and searches for good placement; a monitoring module watches rainfall forecasts and starts a run and an alert when a threshold is crossed. A preloaded national data layer includes about 1.1 million road segments, 65,909 schools, 35,561 hospitals, 101 dams and 3,378 glacial lakes. We evaluate the platform without flattering it. In a controlled comparison on three 13 km areas of the 2022 Sindh flood, scored against the UNOSAT satellite flood extent, SAILAB’s mean critical success index (CSI) is 0.504, against 0.503 for ANUGA, 0.500 for SFINCS and 0.497 for TELEMAC-2D, and its flooded cells overlap those of ANUGA and TELEMAC-2D by 0.94 and 0.90, which supports the correctness of the numerics. But no model, SAILAB included, beats the trivial forecast “flood everything” (CSI 0.500), and ranking cells by simulated depth is barely better than ranking them by elevation alone (mean ROC AUC 0.57 against 0.59). We therefore claim solver agreement and workflow integration, not predictive skill, for rainfall-only forcing of this event. The solver handled 4.4 million cells in 98 s on one GPU and was 6.9 times faster than one CPU thread at 0.7 million cells; a complete run for a 13 km area, with buildings, roads, risk, loss and a 3D scene, took 81–187 s.

Keywords: flood inundation modelling; shallow-water equations; GPU computing; flood damage; nature-based solutions; early warning; Pakistan; Indus basin.

1 Introduction

Pakistan’s 2022 floods affected about 33 million people and caused more than US$30 billion in damage and economic loss, about 70 % of it in Sindh [1]. The event showed a recurring gap between what decision makers need and what their tools provide. The owner, lender or planner of an asset asks five questions: will water reach the site; how deep and how fast will it be; what will the damage cost; which protection actually pays back; and will we know before it happens.

Each question is normally answered by a different tool. Hazard maps come from a hydraulic model run by a specialist and are usually a handful of fixed return periods. Damage is estimated later, in a GIS or a spreadsheet. Mitigation is assessed by running the hydraulic model again, often weeks later. Forecasts live in a separate meteorological system. Because every hand-off costs time, few scenarios are tested and results are rarely refreshed when conditions change.

This paper describes SAILAB, built at AI Geo Navigators and launched at Bahria University, Islamabad, on 16 November 2026. Its contribution is integration rather than a new numerical scheme: one engine and one data record serve simulation, quantification, protection and monitoring. We also report what the platform does not yet show. Our contributions are:

  1. A two-stage workflow in which a terrain-based instant preview screens scenarios and a GPU physics run confirms the ones that matter, with measured runtimes and a measured, and modest, agreement between the two stages.
  2. A building-level damage and rupee-loss model, with annual average loss, driven directly by simulated depth.
  3. A mitigation module that evaluates engineered and nature-based options and searches for placement before capital is committed.
  4. A monitoring module that couples forecast rainfall to automatic simulation and alerting, with measured latencies.
  5. A preloaded national data layer, and a controlled comparison of the solver against three independent flood models on the 2022 Sindh flood, including a threshold-free skill test. The comparison supports the solver’s correctness but shows no predictive skill for rainfall-only forcing, and we say so.

Paper structure. Section 2 reviews related work and Section 3 gives the system overview. Sections 4 to 7 describe the engine, exposure and damage, mitigation and monitoring. Section 8 reports the evaluation and sensitivity, Section 9 reports runtime, Section 10 presents a protection case study, and Sections 11 and 12 discuss limits and conclude.

2 Background and related work

2.1 Hydraulic flood inundation modelling

Two-dimensional hydraulic models solve the shallow-water equations, or simplified forms of them, on a gridded terrain. Reduced-complexity formulations such as the local-inertial scheme of Bates et al. [2] made large-domain simulation affordable and underpin continental and global hazard models [3, 4]. The LISFLOOD-FP code implements that scheme with sub-grid channel options [5] and, since version 8.1, GPU solvers [6]. SFINCS applies a reduced-physics formulation to compound coastal flooding [7]. Full finite-volume and finite-element solvers such as ANUGA [8] and TELEMAC-2D [9] solve the complete equations at higher cost. Terrain-based screening methods such as Height Above the Nearest Drainage (HAND) [10] give fast, depth-only estimates without dynamics; large-scale variants map inundation over whole catchments from a rating curve and a terrain index [11]. SAILAB combines the two ends: a terrain-based preview for screening and a full solver for confirmation.

2.2 Global and national flood hazard products

Global hazard models give consistent maps at tens of metres to kilometre resolution for fixed return periods [3]. Machine-learning streamflow forecasting now reaches ungauged basins worldwide [12], and the Global Flood Awareness System provides ensemble river-discharge forecasts and a long reanalysis [13, 14]. Such products are static or coarse, are not tied to the local building stock, and cannot test a user-defined intervention. For 2022 specifically, studies attribute the Pakistan event mainly to multiday extreme monsoon rainfall on wet soil, with glacier melt a smaller factor [15], and attribute the scale of the impacts to high exposure and vulnerability [16]. Because that rain fell over a very large catchment, how rainfall-only simulations should be read needs care (Section 8).

2.3 Damage and loss

Depth–damage functions relate water depth to the fraction of an asset’s value lost. SAILAB uses the Asia-region residential and agricultural functions and maximum damage values of the JRC global database [17]; Section 5 gives the details and the limits of using a continental average for Pakistan.

2.4 Early warning

Operational flood warning chains connect a forecast, a hydrological model and a communication channel. SAILAB’s monitoring module automates the first link, from forecast to simulation, and delivers an alert to the user’s team; it does not replace official warnings (Section 11.3).

3 System overview

3.1 Four steps on one engine

SAILAB organises work in four steps (Figure 1): simulate, quantify, protect and monitor. The user defines an area (a drawn polygon, an uploaded boundary, a tehsil or a river basin), configures a flood type and size, runs a preview and then a full simulation, and decides using depth, damage and loss outputs. Outputs are an interactive 3D view, maps, an animation, PDF reports and alerts.

3.2 Supported flood types

Six forcing types are supported. Each reaches the same solver in one of two ways: water falls on every cell as rain, or it enters through a boundary as a discharge or a stage (Table 2).

3.4 Software and compute architecture

SAILAB is a Python 3.12 web service built on Flask, with a browser front end on MapLibre/Mapbox GL for 2D and 3D maps. A run is a background job. Jobs share a single solver slot and queue in order; the queue position, the job that holds the slot and a cancel control are visible to the user. Results are cached by a hash of the area geometry, all parameters and a fingerprint of the data used, so an identical request returns in milliseconds and a change in any input produces a fresh run. The flood solver is run as a subprocess; national vector data are read from a PostGIS 3.6 database with the original files as fallback. Reports are rendered to PDF with WeasyPrint. Background agents check the health of the data sources every 15 minutes, score logged forecasts against later analyses and screen each finished run for implausible results (non-finite values, depth above 100 m, large mass-balance error, almost the whole domain flooded). There is no container image: the service runs from a Python virtual environment and is deployed by a patch package. A Python client package exposes the same runs to other systems.

All benchmarks in this paper ran on one NVIDIA GB10 (DGX Spark class, compute capability 12.1; driver 580.178.04, CUDA 13.0) on a 20-core ARM64 server shared with other users. The load average was about 30 during the benchmarks, so timings include that contention. Code state: commit 083ed51 of the project repository; evaluation scripts are in the paper/ folder (Appendix A).

4.2 Solver

SAILAB uses LISFLOOD-FP 8.2, an established, peer-reviewed, open-source (GPL-3.0) solver from the University of Bristol and collaborators [2, 5, 6]. We built it from source for CUDA 13 on an ARM64 Blackwell-class GPU, wrapped it for automatic set-up, and changed the following:

  • Build compatibility. Four source fixes for a 2022-era code base on CUDA 13 (C++17 standard, a cbrt macro that corrupted CUDA’s own declaration, removed device-property fields, a sync() name clash).
  • An upstream argument bug in the full-equation GPU solvers. The max-depth buffer was created for the wrong solver variant, so it was too small for DG2/FV runs; this caused an illegal-address crash at the first step on domains of about half a million cells and larger (250,000 cells ran, 490,000 crashed). With the one-argument fix a 4.0 million-cell DG2 run completed.
  • Stage boundaries. The GPU local-inertial kernel applied a stage (tide) boundary only to edge cells that were already wet, so a dry coast received no water. We changed the condition on all four edges. On a Karachi coastal test the GPU result matches the CPU build (41.4370 km², 4.00 m maximum depth, both) and runs about three times faster (34 s against 97 s).
  • Constant inflow. The GPU kernel has no constant-inflow case, so a constant discharge is written as a flat two-point time series.
  • Outputs the GPU path does not write. The hazard raster and the cumulative-rainfall column are computed by the wrapper from the per-step depth and velocity files and from the rainfall file.
  • Threading. CPU runs are pinned to one thread: in a controlled test, two to twenty OpenMP threads were six to seven times slower than one, apparently because of a critical section inside the parallel loop.

Grid resolution is that of the terrain (about 29 m). The time step is adaptive (α=0.7), starting at 5 s, but at 0.2 s for runs with a river or outburst inflow and 1.0 s on terrain steeper than 10 % mean slope, where the larger step was found to blow up. Flux across a face is switched off below a depth of 1 mm, and a cell counts as wet in outputs above 5 cm. The platform currently uses the local-inertial solver for every run (a setting chosen for speed); the full-equation DG2 solver can be selected from the command line and is used for steep-terrain tests. Where the local-inertial solver is run on terrain steeper than 10 % mean slope, depths are divided by an empirical factor, 2.97 + 0.037 × (mean slope in %), fitted to seven comparisons with DG2 (R² = 0.992); it corrects magnitude only, not flow paths.

Verification. On a 104 km² benchmark the port gives bit-identical results to the original CPU release (v5.9) on the CPU, and the GPU run differs from the CPU run by 0.003 m in maximum depth (2.816 m against 2.818 m; 31,896 against 31,924 flooded cells) while taking about 2 s against 24 s. Section 8 adds a comparison with three independent codes.

4.3 Instant preview

The preview does not run the solver. It computes, from the terrain alone, flow directions and accumulation (D8, after filling depressions), a stream network where at least 500 cells drain in, and the height of every cell above its nearest stream (HAND) [10]. For rain of intensity i over catchment area A the flow in the channel follows the rational method with runoff coefficient C=0.5,

(4)

and the depth in each stream cell is found from Manning’s equation in a trapezoidal channel whose width follows a hydraulic-geometry relation, W=0.6A0.5 [34, 35]. A cell is flooded where its HAND is below the stage of its nearest stream cell. Rain that falls into closed depressions fills them from the lowest point up to the spill level. A library of 12 discharge levels is stored per area so that a slider interpolates between levels in the browser without any server call. Development logs give 0.34 s for a first request on a small Pakistani area and 0.014 s when the terrain analysis is cached; we did not benchmark large areas for this paper.

How close is the preview to the full run? We compared it with the full solver on 315 terrain windows of 128 × 128 cells (3.7 km), spread over Pakistan, each simulated with a random storm (8–120 mm/h, 1–6 h), roughness (0.02–0.15) and infiltration (0–20 mm/h). The preview ignores roughness and infiltration, as the instant page does. Agreement is modest (Table 4, Figure 3).

4.4 Forcing and boundary conditions

  • Rainfall. A triangular storm (peak at half the duration) from a peak intensity and a duration; an uploaded station series (for example 15-minute gauge data) used point by point; or a return-period storm. Return-period depths come from a Gumbel fit to annual-maximum daily rainfall of CMIP6 historical simulations bias-corrected by the NEX-GDDP method (1985–2014, ensemble median of up to five models) [19]; the 24-hour depth becomes a 24-hour triangular storm with the same total. Real intensity–duration curves from ERA5-Land and IMERG [18] are available for design intensities at shorter durations. Rain is applied uniformly over the area unless an areal-reduction option is switched on.
  • Losses. A constant infiltration rate, scaled by an antecedent-moisture slider (multipliers 1.8, 1.0 and 0.25 for dry, average and saturated soil, interpolated between), or an area-mean saturated conductivity from soil texture [31, 32]. The GPU solver takes one loss rate per run, so the soil-derived value is an area mean, not a per-cell field.
  • Roughness. Manning’s n per cell from land cover: tree cover 0.130, mangroves 0.140, shrubland 0.070, cropland 0.037, grassland 0.035, wetland 0.045, built-up 0.045, snow 0.030, bare ground 0.025, water 0.025 (typical overland-flow values [35]). The user value, default 0.05, is used where land cover is missing. Buildings are burned into the terrain as obstacles using their full footprints.
  • Boundaries. All four edges are open by default (free outflow, slope 0.001); a closed basin can be chosen. Inflows and stage boundaries are described in Table 2.

5 Exposure, damage and loss

5.1 Hazard metrics extracted from each run

For every cell the solver stores the peak depth h, the time of first wetting and the total wet time, and per time step the depth and the face velocities. The speed |u| = (qx² + qy²)1/2/h is computed after averaging the staggered velocity components back to cell centres. A hazard rating is the peak over time of a simplified form of the Defra/Environment Agency rating for people [36],

(5)

banded as low (below 0.75), moderate (0.75–1.25), significant (1.25–2.0) and extreme (above 2.0). The depth–velocity product is not stored separately.

5.2 Building damage classes

Each building’s peak depth is sampled at its footprint centroid and converted to a damage fraction D(h) with the JRC Asia residential curve [17], linearly interpolated: 0 at 0 m, 0.30 at 0.5 m, 0.45 at 1 m, 0.55 at 1.5 m, 0.62 at 2 m, 0.72 at 3 m, 0.80 at 4 m, 0.86 at 5 m and 0.90 at 6 m. The class follows from the fraction: minor below 0.30 (depth under 0.5 m), moderate 0.30–0.55 (to 1.5 m), major 0.55–0.72 (to 3 m) and severe above, with dry for depths of 5 cm or less. A damage class built on absolute depth was chosen over depth relative to building height because the usual single-storey height assumption made almost every real flood look minor.

5.3 Loss in rupees and annual average loss

Structure loss of building k is its floor area times the maximum damage value times the damage fraction, and contents add a fixed share,

(6)

with footprint area Ak from the building polygon, the number of storeys sk = max(1, height/3 m), and the JRC Asia residential maximum damage value V, 111 EUR/m² at 2010 prices [17]. The content ratio of 50 % of structure loss is a common residential assumption in US flood-loss practice. Buildings without a recorded height are given 6 m, that is two storeys, which doubles their floor area relative to a single storey; we state this because it moves the totals. Pakistan has no country-specific value in the JRC table, so the Asia average is used. Euros are converted at a current mid-market rate (322.5 PKR/EUR, September 2026) and are not adjusted for inflation since 2010, so loss figures are indicators for comparing options rather than forecasts of cost. Cropland adds area × damage fraction × the JRC agriculture value (0.03 EUR/m²); built-up land is not priced a second time.

For events with annual exceedance probabilities pk (p1 > p2 > …) and losses Lk, the annual average loss is the area under the loss–exceedance curve,

(7)

by the trapezoid rule. Beyond the rarest return period tested the loss is held constant, since a rarer event is not assumed cheaper; below the most frequent one it falls linearly to zero. We use return periods of 10, 50 and 100 years from the design storms above (Section 10).

5.4 Critical facilities and access

Schools, hospitals, power lines, railway lines, airports, ports and, inside Islamabad, shelters, police posts, water and fuel facilities are sampled at their centroids: a facility is flooded if the peak depth exceeds 5 cm there, and its hazard band is read from the hazard raster. Road segments are treated the same way, so disruption is graded by hazard band and not a binary passability test. Roads are further split into major (motorway to tertiary) and local, with kilometres flooded and bridges counted; the terrain model has no bridge decks, so a bridge counts as flooded when the ground beneath it is, which makes bridge counts an upper bound.

6 Mitigation module

6.1 Options

Every option changes the inputs of the same solver; nothing is a separate model.

  • Embankment or barrage. A user-drawn line is buffered into a corridor (half-width 8 m and 12 m by default) and the terrain is raised there by the design height (3.0 m and 2.5 m). Square caps tie the ends into higher ground.
  • Drainage pipe or diversion canal. The corridor (half-width 1.5 m and 6 m) is lowered by 1.0 m and 2.0 m. The drain is a one-dimensional approximation: a lowered channel in the terrain, not a closed pipe network.
  • Retention basin. A wide lowered footprint that captures runoff and overflows when full, as a real detention basin does; its storage volume is reported as an upper bound.
  • Nature-based options. The land cover of the whole area is changed to forest, wetland or grassland, immediately (active) or by one succession step (passive). This changes the roughness grid and the area-mean infiltration rate.

Structures are applied to the same building-conditioned terrain as the baseline, so the effect of buildings is the same in both runs. Dimensions are defaults, not engineering designs.

6.2 Placement optimiser

The optimiser searches for the placement of one to three structures that minimises a loss-weighted objective: the negative percentage of baseline building loss avoided, plus half the percentage of cells that become newly flooded because water was displaced, so a design that merely moves the problem next door is penalised. Each structure has seven decision variables: centre position (two), orientation, length, a height multiplier, a width multiplier and the structure type. The search uses differential evolution [37] as implemented in mystic [38], with a seed derived from the request so identical requests repeat. Every candidate is evaluated with a full solver run, not with the instant preview, because the preview cannot represent a structure’s effect on flow. The budget is therefore small and user-chosen: 12, 24 or 48 trials (Quick, Balanced, Thorough). The best evaluated trial is reported rather than the solver’s final vector, so the reported metrics always match the geometry shown.

7 Monitoring and alerting

A user registers a site (a drawn area or a point), a rainfall threshold in mm/h, a check interval (3 hours by default) and, optionally, a webhook address. A background scheduler wakes every 10 minutes and checks each site that is due. A check fetches the Open-Meteo hourly forecast (three days ahead, three days back) [20], finds the peak hourly rate in the future part of the series and the contiguous rain event around it, and compares the peak with the threshold. If it is crossed, the platform submits a full physics run through its own run endpoint, using the forecast peak and duration as a triangular storm, and posts an alert. The alert goes to any Slack-, Discord-, ntfy- or custom-style webhook with the site, peak, duration, threshold and run identifier, and includes a report link when the platform has a public address. Forecast times are in the site’s local time zone, and “now” is computed in that same zone; a UTC comparison once misplaced the future–past boundary by five hours for Pakistan and was fixed.

The module is deliberately simple. It uses one deterministic forecast and a single threshold, with no ensemble or probability, so it can both miss a localised burst that a 10 km model does not resolve and raise an alert for rain that does not fall. Every logged forecast is scored after 54 hours against a later analysis from the same provider, so a false-alarm and miss rate can be measured; we have not yet analysed that ledger. The alert is sent when the run is submitted, not when it finishes, so the first message carries a run identifier and the results follow.

8 Evaluation: solver verification and a skill test on the 2022 Sindh flood

We ask two separate questions. First, does SAILAB’s solver give the same answer as independent flood models fed the same inputs? That tests correctness of the numerics. Second, does the simulated extent match what a satellite saw? That tests predictive skill. The two have different answers.

8.1 Design

We simulated the 2022 Sindh flood and scored the result against the flood extent that UNOSAT derived from VIIRS imagery of 29 November 2022 (product FL20221121PAK) [39]. The product is a binary flood-water polygon layer with no depths, about 4,663 km² within a 46,150 km² analysis extent, observed three months after the August peak, so it shows water that persisted. VIIRS pixels are about 375 m, so the observed mask is blocky next to a 29 m model (Figure 6).

  • Areas. Three 13 km boxes placed on the edge of the observed flood so that each is about half flooded (49, 50.5 and 51 %; Figure 4). Placing them on the edge is what makes the test discriminating: a box that is almost entirely flooded or dry cannot show skill.
  • Inputs, identical for every model. SRTM terrain (29 m), 253.5 mm of rain over 48 hours (the wettest 48-hour window of the 2022 monsoon in ERA5 at a site in the Manchar–Dadu belt [40], applied to all three areas as a triangular storm with peak 10.56 mm/h), Manning’s n = 0.05, no infiltration, closed boundaries, no buildings. Nothing was calibrated to the observed extent: these values were fixed in advance.
  • Scoring. A cell is wet above 5 cm of peak depth. We report CSI, POD and FAR (Equations below), and threshold-free measures (Section 8.5).
  • Baselines. “Flood everything”, and “lowest terrain”, which floods the lowest cells up to the observed flooded fraction (it is told the answer’s size).
  • Models. SAILAB (LISFLOOD-FP 8.2, local-inertial, GPU); ANUGA 4.1 (full equations, finite volume, 90 m mesh) [8]; SFINCS (local-inertial, 29 m) [7]; TELEMAC-2D (full equations, finite element, 90 m mesh, one core) [9].

8.2 A withdrawn result

Draft v0.1 of this paper reported CSI 0.659, POD 0.669 and FAR 0.021 for one 11 km box centred on the largest contiguous flooded polygon, and described it as good agreement. We withdraw that interpretation. In that box 98.1 % of the area is flooded in the observation, so a forecast of “flood everywhere” would have scored POD 1.0 and CSI 0.98; SAILAB’s 0.659 was worse than the trivial forecast. A box that is nearly all flooded cannot show skill (Appendix B). The same run also used half the intended rainfall (127 mm, because the average rate had been passed as the triangle’s peak); the figures below use the corrected storm.

8.6 Where the model misses, and why we think so

We have not isolated the cause, and list candidates in order of how much we suspect them. (1) Forcing. Studies attribute the 2022 event mainly to multiday extreme rainfall over a very large area [15]; much of that water reached Sindh as river flow and standing water rather than as local rain, and a simulation with local rain only cannot represent water that arrives down the Indus system or through breaches. (This is our reading, not a finding of those studies.) The reanalysis rainfall of 253.5 mm is also a coarse ERA5 value. In the GloFAS reanalysis the nearest river cells to our earlier test area carry up to about 17,000 m³/s on 17–31 August 2022 [14]. We did not impose any inflow here, so this is untested. (2) Observation. The extent was seen three months after the peak, at 375 m, and can include irrigated or ponded land that no storm model would flood. (3) Terrain. SRTM is a surface model with metre-scale errors in flat delta land, enough to decide whether land floods; the dry strip in area A is one candidate. (4) Missing structures. Canals, embankments and barrages are not in the terrain. Because the models agree with each other, none of these points to a defect in one solver; they point to the inputs.

11 Discussion

11.1 What the evaluation does and does not show

It shows that SAILAB’s solver, fed the same terrain, rainfall and roughness, produces the same flooded cells as three independent codes, and that a complete run takes minutes. It does not show that rainfall-only SAILAB predicts where the 2022 Sindh flood stood: no model did, and depth added nothing to elevation. The test is one event, one product and one forcing dataset; satellite extents carry their own error (cloud, timing, 375 m pixels), and a single score hides spatial structure. We therefore treat the maps and the skill measures as part of the result.

11.2 Limitations

  • Terrain. SRTM is a surface model at 29 m with a specified absolute vertical error of 16 m (90 %) [27]; in flat delta land, decimetre errors decide whether land floods. Buildings are added as obstacles on top of a surface that may already include them.
  • Forcing. Reanalysis rainfall is coarse and can under-represent local convective peaks; a triangular storm applied uniformly is a simplification.
  • Inflow and structures. Rainfall-only runs omit upstream river inflow, canals, breaches and barrages. River and outburst inflows are supported but there is no channel bathymetry, so a large discharge forced into a channel-width segment can give locally implausible depths.
  • Calibration. No parameter was tuned to the observed extent and no observed discharge was used. (A separate calibration tool exists for users with an observed map; it was not used here.)
  • Solver. The local-inertial solver degrades on extreme relief: on a Gilgit-Baltistan basin with a maximum slope of 852 % it produced a maximum depth of 255 m where the full-equation solver gave 0.83 m on a four-times coarser grid. The empirical depth correction for steep terrain fixes magnitude only. The full-equation solver is about 60 times slower. On one 2022 test area the CPU build reported a larger maximum depth than the GPU build at a few sink cells (16.3 m against 8.95 m at one cell) while the wet footprint matched; the cause is not explained.
  • Instant preview. It over-predicts area and depth (Section 4.3).
  • Damage. Depth–damage functions and values are a continental average at 2010 prices converted at a current exchange rate; heights default to 6 m; centroid sampling. Loss figures compare options and should not be read as forecasts of cost.
  • Dams and glacial lakes. The outburst peak from a volume relation had a median ratio to observed of 1.05 over 16 moraine-dammed events but a 10–90 % range of 0.11 to 14.3, so it is an order of magnitude estimate; documented events are used where they exist. Dam-breach peaks come from a relation fitted to small dams, and for 64 of 101 dams the height is estimated. A spillway-release outlook from GloFAS was checked only for Tarbela (6,576 m³/s against documented 6,881 and 5,947) and is refused for Mangla (GloFAS 2 m³/s against a documented 8,495).
  • Snowmelt. A distributed melt model agrees well with GloFAS for one Hunza basin after calibration (KGE 0.95; 0.87 on a held-out year) but cannot be reconciled for a neighbouring basin; GloFAS is itself a model, not gauges, and parameters do not transfer between basins.
  • Coastal. One tide gauge (Karachi) serves the whole coast. An earlier version applied return levels measured on the gauge scale to the terrain as if they were above mean sea level, overstating flooding; the datum was corrected on 14 October 2026 and results produced before then with that source overstate flooding.
  • Runtime. Times are from a shared server and single runs.

11.3 Ethical and operational considerations

Automated alerts can cause harm if wrong or ignored. SAILAB is a decision-support tool; its alerts do not replace official warnings from the national authorities. 11.4 Towards global coverage

The architecture is not specific to Pakistan; the data layer, forcing sources and damage inputs are. The platform is deliberately limited to Pakistan: since 20 November 2026 areas outside the country are refused and no terrain is fetched from the internet. An earlier prototype that fetched Copernicus GLO-30 terrain worked on a Nairobi area, because buildings (Google Open Buildings), roads (OpenStreetMap), land cover (WorldCover), soils (SoilGrids) and the reanalysis forcing are already global; that option was removed. Extending beyond Pakistan again would need, in order: (1) a terrain choice per region with a stated vertical error; (2) regional damage functions and maximum-damage values, which the JRC database provides by continent; (3) region-specific hazard inputs (outburst inventories, dam registers, tide and surge records); (4) a validation event for each region, since the evidence in this paper is for one Pakistani event.

12 Conclusion

SAILAB puts flood simulation, building-level loss, mitigation testing and forecast-triggered monitoring on one engine and one data record, and a complete run for a 13 km area takes minutes. Its solver agrees closely with three independent flood models on the same inputs, which is good evidence that the numerics are right. On the 2022 Sindh flood, however, rainfall-only simulation, by SAILAB or by any of the other models, did not reproduce the observed extent better than flooding everything, and its depth pattern carried no information beyond elevation. We attribute this, without having tested it, to missing river inflow and to terrain and observation limits. The next steps are therefore clear: impose upstream inflow, test a second event, calibrate against observed extents with a held-out event, and complete the sensitivity and case-study runs reported as pending here. The platform’s value today is in making those experiments, and the scenario testing that decisions need, quick to run.

B Metric identities

From the definitions in Equation (9), CSI−1 = POD−1 + (1 − FAR)−1 − 1. For a forecast that floods every cell, POD = 1 and FAR = 1 − p, where p is the observed flooded fraction, so CSI = p. In the withdrawn 11 km box p = 0.981, so the trivial forecast scores 0.981 and the reported 0.659 is below it. In the three test areas p is 0.49 to 0.51, so the trivial score is about 0.5 and a score near 0.5 shows no skill. The ROC AUC is the probability that a randomly chosen observed-wet cell has greater simulated depth than a randomly chosen observed-dry cell, with ties counted half; the Peirce score is POD − POFD and the Heidke score is the accuracy gain over chance.
